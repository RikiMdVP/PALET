# RQ5 — Immigration and labour mobility in filling skill gaps during the transition

## 1. What the four studies say

The only empirical study of migrant labour in the green economy is Gregson et al. (2014), who show that resource
recovery (recycling) in the EU is a new form of dirty work located in secondary labour markets and reliant on
itinerant and migrant labour, often from accession states, which local workers are reluctant to take; when waste
stays within the EU, labour moves to process it. The green economy, in this reading, creates stigmatised jobs
filled by intra-EU mobility rather than skilled jobs filled by targeted immigration.

Two studies treat immigration as a response to a supply deficit. Kozera (2024) finds in Polish data on age
structure and education a growing labour-supply deficit and a possible shortage of the competencies needed to green
the economy, and concludes that the shortfall would have to be covered from external resources, that is, labour
migration. Khandii et al. (2025) draw on EU experience, singling out Germany's combination of skilled-immigration
programmes with digital transformation and Poland's diaspora reintegration, to recommend that post-war Ukraine
combine vocational reform, targeted immigration policy and technology-driven productivity measures.

Bisong and Ahairwe (2024) argue at the level of external policy: Europe can fill its demographically driven labour
gap by attracting Africa's young, trained workforce, and recommend linking labour mobility to the green and digital
transitions through bilateral partnerships and skills-oriented education platforms that also create jobs for
African youth.

## 2. Reading across the studies (for the authors to confirm)

- The evidence base is the thinnest of the seven questions: four core studies, one of them empirical on migrants
  in green jobs, one on a national supply deficit, two policy essays (one about Ukraine, one about Europe–Africa
  cooperation). No study estimates the contribution of immigration or intra-EU mobility to filling green or
  digital skill gaps, and none evaluates a mobility instrument.
- The two findings that can be stated: (i) migrant labour already fills the low end of the green economy
  (Gregson); (ii) demographic decline turns immigration into a stated necessity for the green transition in
  Poland (Kozera), with Germany's skilled-immigration programmes cited as the model (Khandii).
- Related material outside RQ5's core set: Istudor et al. (RQ2) note that brain drain and start-up migration leave
  CEE AI graduates underutilised; Kainz (RQ2) links automation to managed labour migration. Both could be brought
  in by the cross-reading as the mobility dimension of the digital transition.
