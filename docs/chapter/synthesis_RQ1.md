# RQ1 — Economic-policy recommendations to mitigate job destruction and boost job creation in the green transition

Draft synthesis for the chapter (ISABEL), written from `runs/isabel_chapter_v1/report/evidence_by_rq.md`
(run `isabel_chapter_v1`, protocol v2, core studies only). Every statement below rests on the abstract of the
study cited, as charted by the model and verified by quote; **check each study against its full text before the
claim goes into the chapter**. Studies: 40. Order within each theme: roughly by the strength of the claim, not by
citations.

## 1. Aggregate employment effects: modest net gains, strong sectoral reallocation

The macro-modelling literature converges on a modest, usually positive, net employment effect of decarbonisation,
with large gross flows between sectors. Pollitt et al. (2014) estimate that a 40 % GHG cut in the EU could add up
to 0.7 million jobs, and up to 1.2 million when combined with renewables and efficiency targets, depending on how
the policy is funded; they read this as a case for keeping all three targets in the EU climate and energy package.
Fragkos and Fragkiadakis (2022) find that well-below-2 °C decarbonisation modestly affects total employment but
sharply cuts jobs in fossil-fuel supply and energy-intensive industries while creating them in low-carbon
manufacturing and electricity. Emmerling et al. (2025) project EU energy-sector employment rising from 1.3 million
today to over 2 million under current policies and 2.5–3 million under a Net Zero pathway by 2050, but stress
that mismatches in skills, geography and timing require more targeted policy than currently provided. At the
national level, Callonnec et al. (2025) find for France that job creation in green industries far exceeds job
destruction in fossil-intensive sectors, with employment 2.8 % above baseline in 2050 under carbon taxation with
full redistribution; Pastore and Santoli (2025) estimate 1.4 million new jobs for a fully renewable Italian
energy system, about three green jobs per job lost, provided planning steers investment toward a just transition.

Three studies qualify these aggregates. Weitzel et al. (2022) show that EU 2030 pathways (regulatory standards,
carbon pricing or a mix) have heterogeneous impacts across sectors, skill types and income groups, which calls for
attention in policy design and complementary measures. Javed and Usman (2025) find in Italy that stricter
environmental policy and the renewable transition raise demand for high-skilled labour and may lower it for
low-skilled labour in traditional energy sectors, and recommend strengthening the social safety net during the
transition. Pociovălişteanu et al. (2015) find small but positive employment effects of EU environmental policy
and argue that environmental and employment policy should be linked more tightly through macro- and
microeconomic measures.

Several studies compare instruments. Ven et al. (2022) find across three energy-economic models that green
recovery packages placing more than half of investment in solar photovoltaics best combine emission cuts and green
jobs, particularly in the EU. Mikulić and Keček (2021) find for Croatia that building retrofits generate more
employment per unit of investment than renewable plants, that the two are complements rather than substitutes, and
that energy-transition jobs could reach 0.5–1 % of national employment. Philippidis et al. (2023) estimate that
publicly backed fiscal support for EU bio-based industries, costing 0.22–0.25 % of GDP by 2050, notably raises
production and employment in bio-based chemicals. Lavecchia and Stagnaro (2014) ask whether Italian solar
subsidies created more jobs than they destroyed through higher electricity prices; the abstract does not report
the answer (see notes).

## 2. Where the losses fall: regional and sectoral concentration

A second group locates the costs. Almazán-Gómez et al. (2024) estimate that fully substituting coal and lignite in
the EU27 causes a net loss of about 95,000 jobs, concentrated in Eastern regions, with gains in regions that host
alternative energy sectors; just-transition support should therefore target the most affected regions. Perdana and
Vielle (2024) find that the employment losses of Fit for 55 fall disproportionately on regions with large energy
mining industries; a carbon border adjustment reduces the exposure of energy-intensive regions, but some Greek,
Spanish and Italian regions remain vulnerable and need targeted support. Ganter et al. (2024) estimate that an
electrolysis-based hydrogen sector would create around 40,000 jobs in Europe by 2050, unevenly across regions,
and recommend sector-diversified workforce transition plans and multi-skill training. Calvillo et al. (2025) find
that the UK heat-pump rollout creates net jobs, mainly in construction and manufacturing, but that skills shortages
raise wages and limit job creation in some regions, so regional levelling-up must be coordinated with jobs and
skills planning.

Two UK studies warn about funding design for carbon capture. Turner et al. (2021) show that the value of
subsidising capture in Scottish industrial clusters depends on how export demand responds to competitiveness
losses, with employment trade-offs across sectors; Turner et al. (2022) find that a polluter-pays funding of CO2
transport and storage could erode the competitiveness of the four regional clusters, with offshoring and regional
job losses, and temper expectations of green growth from CCS.

Case studies of coal regions are the least optimistic. García et al. (2023) project for the mining areas of León
that renewables would create at most about five thousand jobs per zone, far below the forty-five thousand of coal
at its peak, and would not retain young qualified people. Martin and Broughel (2025) find for Upper Silesia that
projected population decline could partly offset mine-closure losses but that effects vary sharply across
counties, that contractors are highly vulnerable, and that retraining toward energy efficiency, automotive and ICT
may suit miners better than renewables; successful transitions require understanding local skill transferability,
transport infrastructure and fiscal capacity.

## 3. Worker-level costs and compensation

A third strand looks at affected workers and the instruments that cushion them. Haywood et al. (2023) find for
German coal miners that wage losses and reduced job security, not unemployment, drive the lifetime welfare costs
of job loss; faster decarbonisation raises these costs and bespoke labour-market policies could reduce them.
Charlier et al. (2025) find for France that carbon taxes reduce emissions but raise unemployment and depress wages
for workers far from job centres, especially rural and low-income workers, and that compensatory policies such as
employment premiums offset part of the effect. Pollin (2023) argues that just-transition policy should guarantee
fossil-fuel workers' jobs, compensation and pensions, with retraining and relocation as supplementary support,
and that such programmes are affordable for high-income economies. Moreira (2025) documents that Portugal's 2021
coal-plant closures produced a "death valley" of unemployment and delayed regional revitalisation despite promises
of reskilling and investment, and calls for clear governance, social dialogue, timely support and a rethinking of
social security for low-carbon transitions. Im et al. (2024) show that planned social-policy responses to
industrial decarbonisation differ across member states: Denmark relies on social investment, Poland on
compensation, Germany on industrial policy, and Spain distinguishes coal miners from other workers.

## 4. Governance: institutions, social dialogue and industrial strategy

A fourth group treats the governance of the transition as the policy variable. Dupuis et al. (2024) review the
tools German automotive workers and unions have to steer the shift to electric vehicles and derive
recommendations for unions and policymakers on how analogous tools could be strengthened elsewhere. Bösch (2023)
argues that German reforms in education, training and wage setting show the twin transition can be a critical
juncture for moving from a dual to an inclusive employment system, and that institutional reform is necessary for
a just transition. Stroud et al. (2015) argue that in declining industrial regions of Germany and the UK an
effective transition policy for skills and decent jobs is more likely where government builds a reciprocal
relationship with regional employers, workers and their representatives. Doshi and Spencer (2025) conclude from UK
home retrofitting and green steel that no single industrial policy delivers net zero, growth, good jobs and
security at once, and recommend adaptive policymaking, conditional public incentives and collaboration with
businesses and communities. Briguglio and Brown (2018) find a broad consensus among Maltese civil-society actors
that sustainable-energy policymaking affecting green jobs should include employers, trade unions and environmental
NGOs alongside experts. Criado (2023) assesses whether the EU Quality Framework for Anticipation of Change can
secure a fair transition in green restructuring through worker-representative involvement and public social
policy.

Two Spanish studies read the Recovery Plan through this lens. Vaquero et al. (2021) recommend harmonising recovery
plans, extending them beyond the energy sector to related green activities and aligning public, private and other
stakeholders to create green jobs; Arnedo et al. (2021) apply the same framework to the hotel industry and offer
information to improve the sectoral recovery plan and coordinate policymakers and businesses.

## 5. Skills, active labour-market policy and national priorities

The remaining studies formulate recommendations at the level of skills and employment policy. Aceleanu et al.
(2015) conclude from EU youth unemployment that active policies, investment in education and green employment play
a positive role. Neverauskienė and Rakauskienė (2018) identify green economy and green jobs, backed by green-skills
promotion and intensified regional policy, as a priority direction for raising employment in Lithuania. Stoyanova
and Harizanova-Bartos (2015) recommend changing the application conditions and employee-related payments of
Bulgaria's green-jobs funding measure and coordinating it with other measures; in a later study (2016) they
recommend training programmes for green employment, adoption of European best practice, better consulting services
and legal changes. Paşnicu and Ciucă (2020) warn that green procurement could accentuate structural imbalances in
the Romanian labour market, while EU-level data show a positive relationship between environmental protection
expenditure and green jobs. Aubert et al. (2021) show for French dairy that decarbonisation through supply-side
measures alone costs jobs, whereas combining supply, demand and market-organisation policies can maintain farm and
agri-food employment. Volodzkienė and Baležentis (2025) find across the EU-27 that gender-equality progress raises
female employment and green-economy growth with a lag, while productivity gains can temporarily cut women's
employment, and recommend gender-sensitive labour, education and environmental policy.

## Reading across the studies (for the authors to confirm)

- The recommendations cluster into five families: (i) keep the aggregate policy mix broad (renewables and
  efficiency targets alongside emission targets; investment portfolios weighted to solar and retrofits);
  (ii) target support territorially at coal, lignite and energy-intensive regions; (iii) compensate workers
  through wage and pension guarantees, employment premiums and social-security reform rather than only through
  retraining; (iv) build institutions for social dialogue and anticipation of change; (v) invest in green skills
  and active labour-market policy.
- The modelling evidence is more optimistic at the aggregate level than the regional case studies (León, Upper
  Silesia, Portugal), which is itself a finding worth stating.
- Instrument comparisons (Ven; Mikulić and Keček; Philippidis; Turner 2021 and 2022) are few and country-specific.
