# RQ2 — Recommendations on the impact of digitalisation and AI on job creation and destruction

Draft synthesis for the chapter (ISABEL), written from `runs/isabel_chapter_v1/report/evidence_by_rq.md`
(run `isabel_chapter_v1`, protocol v2, core studies only). Every statement rests on the abstract of the study
cited, as charted by the model and verified by quote; **check each study against its full text before the claim
goes into the chapter**. Studies: 38.

## 1. Net employment effects: no mass displacement, but polarisation and reallocation

The European evidence does not support the expectation of net job destruction from digitalisation, but it
documents a reallocation that disadvantages routine and low-skilled work. Evangelista et al. (2014) find that ICT
usage, and above all digital empowerment, has the largest economic effects, especially on employment, and favours
the inclusion of disadvantaged groups; they conclude that digitalisation can drive productivity and employment
growth and that inclusive policies help close the gap. Albanesi et al. (2024) find across 16 European countries
that employment shares have risen in occupations more exposed to AI, particularly where workers are younger and
more skilled, with little relation to relative wages; country differences relate to diffusion, education,
competition and employment protection. Calvano and Calzolari (2025) contrast this European pattern with US
evidence of polarisation, where AI displaced low-skill and production jobs while creating high-wage and STEM
opportunities. Neumann (2024) shows that German regions with very different profiles (manufacturing clusters and
large cities) achieved employment growth despite high automation exposure, and reads this as an argument against
policies that try to "protect" jobs from digitalisation and automation. At the firm level, Bratta et al. (2022)
find that Italy's 2017 fiscal incentive for advanced digital production technologies was effective and that the
subsidised investments correlated positively with employment growth at investing firms.

Other studies document the losers. Capello et al. (2024) find that robotisation in Italian NUTS3 regions displaces
jobs, harming low-skilled workers in non-metropolitan manufacturing regions, where wage inequality between groups
rises. Capello et al. (2025) show that platformisation creates gig jobs and advanced digitalisation creates
creative jobs, together producing job downgrading and persistent polarisation that calls for mitigating policies.
Doorn and Van Vliet (2022) find across 16 European countries that the decline of middle-skill employment raises
involuntary part-time work at the bottom of the labour market, an effect that training and job-creation schemes
mitigate. Traverso et al. (2025) find in Italian firms that automation investments go together with non-standard
flexible contracts and call for protections that preserve automation's efficiency gains. Tóth et al. (2025) find
in Central European and Baltic financial sectors that ICT use raises productivity but is associated with declining
employment, while sectoral investment supports jobs. Kušlys et al. (2025) find the effect of digitalisation on
employment in CEE countries inconsistent, with social protection benefits moderating its negative effects in most
specifications.

## 2. The spatial dimension: digital divides and regional divergence

A second strand places the effects in space. Reveiu et al. (2022) use data on 278 EU regions to identify which
dimensions of regional digital development predicted employment resilience at each stage of the Great Recession.
Guarascio et al. (2025) argue from a cluster analysis of EU regions that AI may bring employment gains where
innovation systems are strong, while structural barriers elsewhere could widen regional disparities and leave
peripheral areas further behind. Zhang et al. (2025) find in Great Britain that demand for AI skills concentrates
in neighbourhoods and districts specialised in IT and scientific industries, producing self-reinforcing spatial
inequalities, and recommend resources for competitiveness and worker capabilities in less developed regions.
Rijnks et al. (2022) show substantial regional heterogeneity in the automation threat to Irish agricultural
workers and conclude that labour-market policy responding to automation should take regional context into
account; Crowley and Doran (2022) find that automation risk differs across Irish towns and is explained by
education, age structure, urban size and industry mix. Suciu et al. (2023) find that advanced digital skills are
concentrated in only some member states, so that emergent countries such as Romania need stronger strategies and
policy mixes for Industry 4.0/5.0.

## 3. Skills and education as the main policy lever

Most recommendations concern skills. Bejaković and Mrnjavac (2020) show a significant correlation between digital
skills and employment rates in the EU and urge governments and employers to promote digital literacy and training
across the whole workforce. Istudor et al. (2024) recommend AI-oriented upskilling and reskilling targeting
above-average hard and soft skills in CEE countries, noting that brain drain and start-up migration leave AI
graduates underutilised. Pirvu et al. (2025) find that ICT tertiary enrolments consistently raise graduate
employability in the EU-27, while the effect of ICT graduates and specialists varies by member state with
absorptive capacity and sectoral saturation. Yevtodyeva (2022) identifies shortages of MINT specialists and of
workers with digital and ICT skills as the challenges German labour-market and education programmes address. Luo
and Yang (2023) argue from the Swiss vocational education and training system that deliberately building social
capital is a practical route to transferring VET policy that delivers skills against automation-driven
displacement. Conde-Ruiz et al. (2024) find that Spanish women are concentrated in degrees prone to automation and
under-represented in AI-exposed degrees, and recommend boosting female STEM participation and adapting curricula
toward AI complementarities. Reme et al. (2025) find in Norwegian population data that growing gender gaps in
occupational routine intensity are concentrated among low-socioeconomic groups and driven mainly by educational
differences, and call for targeted interventions. Bode et al. (2018) find that German workers who are more open,
more emotionally stable and less agreeable are less susceptible to digitalisation, and read this as support for
education and labour policies that strengthen personality traits.

## 4. Social protection, taxation and the regulation of work

A fourth group looks at the welfare state. Greve (2019) expects automation to cut industrial and service jobs,
deepen dualisation and leave workers with obsolete skills unemployed, and proposes adjustments to welfare
financing, active labour-market policy and reduced working hours. Colombino and Islam (2024) find for France,
Germany, Italy and Luxembourg that welfare-optimal tax-transfer rules under status quo, jobless and polarised
labour-demand scenarios beat current rules and resemble a universal basic income or negative income tax with a
near-flat tax. Casas and Román (2023) find that automation drives early retirement among older European workers,
with heterogeneous effects by gender, education and job status, and propose that later statutory retirement ages
be accompanied by training, self-employment support and redeployment into less automatable occupations. Dolado et
al. (2021) argue that Spain faces a large reallocation shock from Covid-19 and technological change that requires
labour reforms and new employment and social policies to smooth reallocation and limit inequality. Kainz (2021)
argues that automation raises employment pressure and drives managed labour migration tied to competencies and
demographic decline, so that workers need more robust legal and social protection. Dinu et al. (2025) discuss
proactive policies for adapting EU labour markets to AI, including retraining and support for affected groups and
sectors.

Two studies look at public preferences. Lee (2024) finds in the UK and Sweden that subjective concern and
occupational risk, combined with weak labour-market safeguards, produce demands to restrict automation and
compensate job loss, so that stronger safety nets and shared benefits moderate public anxiety. Pulkka (2018) finds
that most Finns expect technological unemployment to rise at least temporarily but not to become permanent, and
prefer education reforms over basic income or work sharing.

## 5. Social dialogue and sectoral governance

The remaining studies treat the governance of technological change at sector and workplace level. Ilsøe (2017)
finds that social partners in Denmark, Sweden and Germany are active unilaterally on the digitalisation of
service work, but that their tripartite and bipartite responses to automation and platform work differ across the
three countries. Oder and Béland (2024) recommend, from interviews with low-skilled Austrian call-centre agents
whose emotional labour rose with flawed generative-AI tools, targeted education campaigns, stronger social
dialogue, co-determination rights and tailored upskilling. Pronello and Fedeli (2025) propose, through stakeholder
co-creation, eleven validated policies for a just technology transition in transport, grouped into public
governance and regulation, industrial governance, training and reskilling, and minimising workforce exclusion and
exploitation. Stroud et al. (2021) find at European steel sites that the labour-saving insertion of digitalised
drones is highly contingent on workplace power relations and on social, economic and legal factors. Moniz et al.
(2022) examine how AI and automation are likely to reshape work organisation, employment, skills and industrial
relations in Portugal's automotive sector.

## Reading across the studies (for the authors to confirm)

- The European evidence on AI exposure (Albanesi; Calvano and Calzolari; Neumann) is more benign than the public
  debate: employment grew in exposed occupations and regions. The losses are specific: routine and low-skilled
  workers (Capello 2024; Doorn and Van Vliet), non-metropolitan manufacturing regions (Capello 2024), older
  workers (Casas and Román), women in automatable degrees (Conde-Ruiz; Reme).
- Recommendations cluster into (i) digital and AI skills, from literacy to VET and STEM, with attention to gender
  and to CEE countries; (ii) active labour-market policy (training and job creation) as the instrument that
  measurably mitigates polarisation; (iii) welfare financing, tax-transfer and retirement rules adapted to a
  polarised labour demand; (iv) social dialogue and co-determination at sector and workplace level; (v) regional
  policy that counters self-reinforcing concentration of AI demand.
- The regional strand (Reveiu; Guarascio; Zhang; Rijnks; Crowley and Doran) is diagnostic more than prescriptive:
  it identifies divergence but rarely tests an instrument. This contrasts with RQ1, where regional targeting is an
  explicit recommendation.
- Against RQ1, the digital strand has fewer modelling studies of aggregate effects and more micro and survey
  evidence; the one firm-level policy evaluation (Bratta) is Italian.
