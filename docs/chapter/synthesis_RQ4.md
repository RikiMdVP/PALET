# RQ4 — Initial-education and lifelong-learning models for green and digital skills

Draft synthesis for the chapter (ISABEL), written from `runs/isabel_chapter_v1/report/evidence_by_rq.md`
(run `isabel_chapter_v1`, protocol v2, core studies only). Every statement rests on the abstract of the study
cited, as charted by the model and verified by quote; **check each study against its full text before the claim
goes into the chapter**. Studies: 31 (Bianchi et al. also core to RQ3; Pirvu et al. and Luo and Yang also core to
RQ2).

## 1. The diagnosis: skills gaps, mismatch and the shift to skill-based hiring

Several studies document the gap the education system is asked to close. Bone et al. (2025) find in UK vacancy
data that employers have begun skill-based hiring for AI roles, relaxing qualification requirements to widen the
talent pool, and recommend alternative skill-building formats: apprenticeships, on-the-job training, MOOCs,
vocational education and training, micro-certificates and bootcamps. Costa et al. (2025) find a green-skills gap
in surveys of Irish students and companies and recommend experiential university learning, industry
collaboration, flexible access to green education, company upskilling and public-private partnerships. Ribeiro et
al. (2023) find that European agri-food and forestry stakeholders regard sustainability, soft and digital skills
as key, and that neither formal nor non-formal training covers the need, formal training being better suited to
education requirements. Vuc et al. (2025) find that recent Romanian graduates report limited exposure to
AI-related skills in formal education and a growing need for reskilling. Grama and Todericiu (2025) conclude from
the European literature that higher-education curricula, including Romania's, must be adapted to the mismatch
between graduate qualifications and demand for AI and digital skills. Branca et al. (2022) argue that green-economy
and skills-development policies, with new or revised training programmes, allow energy-intensive industries to
anticipate labour-market change by identifying skill-requirement impacts. Bles et al. (2025) find across European
countries that ICT infrastructure and skill demand are the contextual factors most strongly associated with
adults' ICT proficiency, and that adult education relates mainly to higher proficiency among the already highly
educated. Sanguino et al. (2025) find that holding a master's degree and working in positions requiring at least a
bachelor's degree is associated with better alignment between field of study and job, and propose an AI-supported
training model combining career recommendation, flexible modules and mentoring.

## 2. Vocational education and training: system design matters more than "green skills" packages

The strongest cluster concerns VET. Stroud et al. (2024) argue from Germany and the UK that green goals are better
served by VET systems whose functioning and pedagogy produce broad capabilities than by delivering parcels of
narrowly defined green skills. Evans and Stroud (2014) find that the greening of steel-sector skills through
engineering apprenticeships advances more dynamically in Germany, with robust VET frameworks, than in the UK, where
short-term priorities and a compliance focus hinder investment; institutional context explains the difference.
Luo and Yang (2023) argue from the Swiss system that deliberately building social capital is a practical route to
transferring VET policy that delivers skills against automation-driven displacement. Kiener et al. (2022) find that
Swiss VET graduates trained in curricula combining IT skills with technical or non-technical skills obtain better
labour-market outcomes than other graduates, which they read as a case for integrating digital skills as packages
rather than stand-alone content. Storonyanska et al. (2025) find across EU member states that strong partnerships
with labour-market stakeholders and investment in green and digital transitions make VET systems more responsive,
while the evidence on decentralisation and infrastructure expansion is mixed. Carstensen et al. (2025) find that
Danish vocational programmes integrated green skills unevenly between 2015 and 2024, following four trajectories
(rapid, incremental, limited, and delayed-then-accelerated). Chen (2025) finds that logistics VET curricula in
Germany and China favour general transferable green skills but that integration remains largely cosmetic, so
systemic approaches are needed. Pagkratis (2024) identifies weak integration of education for sustainable
development in German VET and recommends aligning STEM learning outcomes with labour-market needs, including AI,
digitalisation and sustainability. Cook and Elliott (2025) go further on the employer side: government should
formally require companies to provide the necessary training as a condition of state funding for renewable
projects, alongside apprenticeships and in-house training.

## 3. Higher education: curricula, enrolments and unequal returns

A third group looks at universities. Menoyo (2014) proposes four key sustainability competencies with performance
indicators for Spanish university curricula, preparing graduates for green jobs and other sectors. Spada et al.
(2024) mine the EU Taxonomy and ESCO green concepts to identify enabling competencies and priority disciplines,
grouped into six clusters, to guide sustainability-oriented curriculum development. Pirvu et al. (2025) find that
ICT tertiary enrolments consistently raise graduate employability across the EU-27, while the labour-market effect
of ICT graduates and specialists varies by member state with absorptive capacity and sectoral saturation. Cattani
et al. (2025) find that sustainability-oriented degrees shape local graduate labour markets in Italian regions and
can produce unequal outcomes across regions, which they call "green bias". Hrzic et al. (2025) argue that public
health curricula lack digital skills and propose embedding digital competencies across courses, covering leadership
and organisational change, upskilling through continuous professional development, and interdisciplinary training.

## 4. Continuing education and reskilling in specific sectors

Four studies address the health sector. Tischendorf et al. (2024) recommend theory- and research-based educational
frameworks as the basis for nursing curricula and continuing education in digital competences. Jerjes and Majeed
(2025) argue that UK GP training must move from informal adaptation to structured hybrid training, with a national
telehealth competency framework and systematic digital literacy training. Margheri et al. (2025) describe the
BeWell project, an EQF-aligned modular framework with micro-credentials to upskill and reskill health and care
workers in digital and green skills, with flexible validation of non-formal and informal learning. (Hrzic et al.,
above, belongs to the same strand.)

## 5. Active labour-market training, vulnerable groups and place

The remaining studies look at training as labour-market policy. Ribeiro et al. (2025) find that remote digital
training and internship-based digital upskilling are relevant for rural Portuguese NEETs, particularly highly
educated ones facing skills mismatch, although many still need personalised in-person support. Valadas (2025)
finds that institutional weaknesses in Portuguese active labour-market policy and shortcomings in public employment
service training leave vulnerable unemployed people without the skills for stable employment or for the ecological
and digital transitions. Apostolopoulos et al. (2023) find that power-plant workers in Greece and Cyprus are more
optimistic than Bulgarian workers about moving to renewables, that green entrepreneurs worry about investment
rates, and that training providers face varied educational needs. Bianchi et al. (2024) conclude that regions
should design place-based education and training initiatives suited to their socio-economic characteristics, with
Emilia-Romagna as the case. Aru-Chabilan (2020) reviews how Estonia equipped young people with digital skills from
the 1990s Tiger Leap programme to the Lifelong Learning Digital Turn programme. Khandii et al. (2025) draw on EU
dual education, VET reform and lifelong-learning initiatives to recommend that post-war Ukraine combine vocational
reform, targeted immigration policy and technology-driven productivity measures.

## Reading across the studies (for the authors to confirm)

- The question asks which models are "most effective"; almost no study compares models on outcomes. The
  exceptions are Kiener et al. (IT-plus-technical skill packages in Swiss VET), Pirvu et al. (ICT enrolments and
  employability), Cattani et al. (regional returns to green degrees) and Bles et al. (contextual determinants of ICT
  proficiency). The rest describe gaps, curricula or programmes.
- Where the literature does converge: (i) broad capabilities delivered by well-governed VET systems beat narrow
  "green skills" add-ons (Stroud 2024; Evans and Stroud; Chen; Carstensen); (ii) employer involvement, from
  partnerships to conditional funding, is the lever that makes training respond to demand (Storonyanska; Cook and
  Elliott; Costa; Bone); (iii) digital competencies should be embedded across curricula rather than taught in
  stand-alone courses (Hrzic; Kiener); (iv) place matters, both for design (Bianchi) and for returns (Cattani;
  Ribeiro 2025).
- Two equity findings deserve a place: adult education mostly raises the proficiency of the already highly educated
  (Bles), and green degrees can widen regional inequality among graduates (Cattani).
- Overlap with RQ2 (section 3 there) is large; the cross-reading must assign the skills studies to one question.
