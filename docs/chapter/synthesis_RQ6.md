# RQ6 — Designing just-transition policies to prevent green and digital discontent in dependent territories

Draft synthesis for the chapter (ISABEL), written from `runs/isabel_chapter_v1/report/evidence_by_rq.md`
(run `isabel_chapter_v1`, protocol v2, core studies only). Every statement rests on the abstract of the study
cited, as charted by the model and verified by quote; **check each study against its full text before the claim
goes into the chapter**. Studies: 57, the largest set. Six are also core to RQ1 (Emmerling; Martin and Broughel;
Pollin; Almazán-Gómez; García 2023; Moreira) and one to RQ4 (Apostolopoulos).

**The first finding is what is missing**: all 57 studies concern fossil-fuel territories (coal, lignite, peat, oil
and gas, carbon-intensive industry). Not one addresses discontent in territories with low levels of
digitalisation. The digital half of the question has no evidence base in this corpus.

## 1. Discontent is real, measurable and politically consequential

A first group documents the political economy that just-transition policy is meant to defuse. Brauers and Oei
(2020) show that opposition to the Polish coal phase-out stems partly from unemployment fears in coal regions, so
that policies reducing coal must be implemented jointly with social and structural measures for the affected
regions to be politically feasible. Im (2024) finds across West European regions that perceptions of economic and
political unfairness rise only where both CO2 emissions and employment in carbon-polluting sectors have declined,
and concludes that green-transition policy should minimise labour-market disruption. Abraham (2019) notes that
Germany gives relatively generous help to displaced coal workers, yet right-wing populists gain ground in
coal-dependent areas, and warns that corporatist compensation bargaining may weaken international labour
solidarity. Kalt (2021) shows that in Germany the unresolved conflict between labour and climate movements over
competing justice claims weakened challenges to coal and delayed the transition, which points to just transitions
that bridge the jobs-versus-climate divide.

The one causal evaluation of a just-transition policy on political outcomes is Bolet et al. (2023): Spain's 2018
just-transition agreement, which phased out coal mining, supported affected workers and invested in affected
municipalities, raised the incumbent party's vote share in coal-mining municipalities at the 2019 election relative
to similar municipalities, an effect driven by union support for the agreement.

Three studies look at how discontent is voiced. Koukouzas et al. (2025) find that in Greece's lignite phase-out
regional media stressed job losses and local identity while national framing was optimistic, so that media
discourse is a component of just transition that policymakers must consider. Vihalemm (2022) finds that news media
practices in north-east Estonia hinder listening to job-losing miners, and suggests energy policy designed to
engage media so that implementers understand vulnerable groups. Malmberg and Van Veelen (2025) find in a Swedish
steel town that social identity can help workers adapt or fuel resistance, especially among blue-collar workers,
so that just transitions must consider socio-cultural effects alongside economic ones. Kaldellis et al. (2023)
find that young scientists in West Macedonia doubt the speed of the green transition and fear migration and brain
drain, and present their engagement in new green-energy activities as a solution.

## 2. Social dialogue, unions and participation as design principles

The largest cluster treats participation as the core of design. Galgóczi (2020) finds that social dialogue is
central to managing large employment transitions in European energy and automotive sectors and draws conclusions
about the changing role of trade unions and the value of cooperative industrial relations. Abraham (2017) finds
that Germany's IG BCE, with control over its industry and a neo-corporatist tradition, demanded and secured a just
transition for miners, whereas the US UMWA came to reject the concept; militant unions with a corporatist
tradition are best positioned to demand just transitions. Bang et al. (2022) find that in Germany broad
stakeholder representation and transition assistance targeting potential losers were crucial to legitimise the
coal phase-out, whereas in the UK cost-effectiveness took precedence over justice concerns. Gürtler et al. (2021)
find that Canada's coal task force captured and relayed community needs more than Germany's commission, which
relied on broad interest representation and spending for affected regions, workers and industries. Hägele et al.
(2022) conclude from Germany and South Africa that just energy transition pathways need inclusive decision-making,
thorough impact assessment and coordination across actors and governance levels. Raitbaur (2021) describes the
German coal laws (phase-out by 2038, company compensation, structural support for regions and workers) and notes
that stakeholders across politics, science, industry and NGOs were dissatisfied with their social-justice effects
and participation process.

Worker-level evidence qualifies the top-down picture. Banerjee and Schuitema (2022) find from Irish peat workers
that just-transition programmes fail where theory and practice diverge, which they attribute to the absence of a
structured process. Lempinen and Vainio (2023) find that 400 Finnish peat workers experienced distributive,
procedural, restorative and recognition injustices during the peat phase-out. Kortetmäki et al. (2024) find that
Finnish industrial workers see just transition as a regional, intra-factory and worker-level challenge requiring
region-, industry- and group-specific solutions. Lempinen et al. (2025) show how union responses to plant closures
in Finland and the US broadened their agency in wider just-transition agendas. Patin et al. (2025) find in
Hauts-de-France and IJmond that state-led participatory processes failed to engage key actors while unions and
other groups advanced counterplans. Wainwright et al. (2025) conclude from UK worker-led industrial conversion
that equitable decarbonisation requires labour power over wages, conditions and industrial direction through
expanded collective bargaining and place-based alliances. Ruyter et al. (2024) draw lessons for West Midlands
automotive suppliers and workers and call for collaborative research on skills needs. Cantoni and Brisbois (2024)
find that carbon-intensive EU regions with a funded plan for workers' transitions progress further through the
phases of the energy transition and that widely participatory plans run more smoothly than top-down ones.

## 3. The EU instruments: narrow in scope, uneven in allocation

A third group assesses the Just Transition Fund, the Just Transition Mechanism and the territorial plans. Crespy
and Munta (2023) find that the Just Transition Fund and the Social Climate Fund have narrow, reactive objectives
centred on reskilling workers hit by decarbonisation, short of a holistic just transition. Castiella and Cuesta
(2022) argue that EU just-transition policy focuses on compensating coal regions and workers to maintain employment
and that this is too narrow because other groups also lose. Siderius (2025) finds that Just Transition Mechanism
allocation varies regionally, that employment projects are more likely where a social-affairs department is
involved, and that much of the funding targets none of employment, equality or carbon neutrality. Peretto et al.
(2024) find that six coal regions' territorial just transition plans address employment and environmental impacts
but under-address social and demographic impacts and need tailored regional policies. Joosse et al. (2025) find
that Just Transition Fund allocation in northern Sweden supports the dominant steel industry to secure regional
jobs, while residents fear deeper local injustices (public-service decline, housing and labour shortages, Sámi
land tensions). McCauley et al. (2023) stress embedding fairness in the deployment of new renewable jobs and
consider a better-targeted financial mechanism alongside fossil-fuel divestment.

Two studies identify where the instruments should go. Vrontisi et al. (2024) build a hazard-exposure-vulnerability
index that finds 15 EU regions at high socioeconomic risk from the low-carbon transition, all already facing
challenges, and call for dedicated support mechanisms. Almazán-Gómez et al. (2024) estimate about 95,000 net jobs
lost from fully substituting coal and lignite in the EU27, concentrated in Eastern regions, and conclude that the
Just Transition Mechanism should target the most affected regions.

Comparative reviews frame the instrument choice. Krawchenko and Gordon (2021) compare just-transition strategies
across 25 countries, 74 regions and the EU level, develop a typology of instruments to protect workers and
communities, and identify implementation gaps and leading practices; Krawchenko and Gordon (2022) do the same for
three oil-and-gas regions (Taranaki, north-east Scotland, Jutland). Krause et al. (2022) compare pathways in
Germany, Poland, Spain, Sweden and the US across welfare regimes so that policies can be judged by context and
transformative potential.

## 4. Compensation, retraining and who is left out

A fourth group looks at the content of support. Pollin (2023) argues that just-transition policy should guarantee
fossil-fuel workers' jobs, compensation and pensions, with retraining and relocation as supplements, at modest
cost to high-income economies. Madsen et al. (2023) conclude from Denmark's oil and gas phase-out that social
acceptability requires industry-specific and regional compensation and systematic retraining for non-fossil
activities, with time for investment to re-orient. Kolde and Wagner (2021) find that subcontracted lignite workers
in the Rhineland face high job-loss risk with virtually no support policies, and call for targeted measures, civic
input and land-use reform. Martin and Broughel (2025) find for Upper Silesia that contractors are highly vulnerable
and that retraining toward energy efficiency, automotive and ICT may suit miners better than renewables. Moreira
(2025) documents that Portugal's 2021 coal-plant closures produced a "death valley" of unemployment despite
promises of reskilling and investment, and calls for clear governance, social dialogue, timely support and reformed
social security. Janikowska and Kulczycka (2021) recommend extending Silesia's just-transition strategy to women's
labour-market situation, with a hub for women's professional activation. Apostolopoulos et al. (2023) find that
power-plant workers in Greece and Cyprus are more optimistic than Bulgarian workers about moving to renewables.
García (2025) finds that Spain's just-transition agreements quantify job risks and labour income but neglect
indirect job losses and job quality, evaluate impacts late, and have design, diagnosis and participation
limitations. Emmerling et al. (2025) find a policy emphasis on cushioning fossil-fuel phase-out rather than on
facilitating the workforce's transition to renewables.

Three studies warn that "just" is being defined too narrowly. Baasch et al. (2024) find that Bremen stakeholders
frame justice in the hydrogen and steel transition almost exclusively as local job preservation, neglecting global
interdependencies. Szabó and Newell (2024) find that the shift to electric-vehicle manufacturing in Europe keeps
high value-added processes in core countries while semi-periphery states compete to retain employment. LaBelle et
al. (2021) propose a radical energy justice framing for Romania's Jiu Valley to expose who decides how miners are
compensated.

## 5. Regional diagnosis and planning in coal territories

The remaining studies diagnose particular territories. Frantál et al. (2022) find that Czech coal and post-mining
districts have worse labour-market indicators, but that unemployment is driven by peripherality, urbanisation,
density, education and business activity rather than coal decline itself, which questions supporting existing
production structures. Dmochowska-Dudek and Wójcik (2022) find Poland's three lignite regions in different phases
of the adaptive cycle, so that just-transition prospects depend on local conditions. Drobniak et al. (2025) build
labour-market scenarios for Silesia under hard-coal phase-out variants to 2049. Voicu-Dorobanţu et al. (2021)
develop just-transition scenarios for Romania's central coal regions. Tranoulidis et al. (2022) find that Western
Macedonia combines new opportunities with high unemployment and low competitiveness and innovation, the main threat
being depopulation for lack of jobs; Kruczek et al. (2025) find that stakeholders in Western Macedonia and Łódzkie
report job losses, reduced income and limited reskilling, and recommend region-specific strategies with workforce
adaptation investment; Boulogiorgou and Kaldellis (2025) stress the knowledge transfer local communities need to
engage equitably; Kourkouridis et al. (2024) propose an entrepreneurship and culture centre in Megalopolis as a
restructuring intervention. García et al. (2023) project that renewables would create at most five thousand jobs
per zone in León's mining areas and would not retain young qualified people. Hopner et al. (2024) present
Katowice's municipal shift from coal as a lighthouse case for sustainable careers. Aylett (2024) summarises the
argument that low-carbon jobs do not replace high-carbon jobs one for one, so that market forces alone will not
produce a just transition, and prescribes stronger industrial policy, reshoring and a central role for unions.

## Reading across the studies (for the authors to confirm)

- Design principles that recur across countries and methods: (i) couple decarbonisation with social and
  structural measures from the start, not after (Brauers and Oei; Bang; Cantoni and Brisbois); (ii) build
  structured, inclusive participation in which unions are partners, because legitimacy and electoral acceptance
  follow union endorsement (Bolet; Galgóczi; Abraham 2017; Gürtler; Patin); (iii) target support territorially
  with risk indicators (Vrontisi; Almazán-Gómez) and tailor plans to local conditions (Peretto; Dmochowska-Dudek;
  Kortetmäki); (iv) guarantee income and pensions, not only retraining (Pollin; Madsen), and cover groups the
  current instruments miss: subcontractors (Kolde and Wagner; Martin and Broughel), women (Janikowska and
  Kulczycka), the young (Kaldellis), non-coal losers (Castiella and Cuesta); (v) time the support so that no
  "death valley" opens between closure and replacement (Moreira; Madsen).
- The EU instruments are judged narrow (reskilling-centred, coal-centred) and unevenly allocated (Crespy and
  Munta; Castiella and Cuesta; Siderius; Peretto; Joosse). This is the most direct answer to the question and
  links to RQ3.
- Generous compensation does not by itself prevent discontent (Abraham 2019; Raitbaur; Im): perceived unfairness
  tracks job decline, not spending, which is why participation and timing carry so much weight in the literature.
- The digital side of RQ6 is empty. The chapter should state that the "digital discontent" of low-digitalisation
  territories is not studied under a just-transition frame, and bring in the regional-divergence findings of RQ2
  (Guarascio; Zhang; Capello 2024) as the closest evidence.
