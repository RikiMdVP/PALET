## 2. Cross-cutting conclusions

**C1. Aggregate optimism, local pessimism.** Macro-models of the green transition find modest, usually positive net
employment effects with large sectoral reallocation (Pollitt; Fragkos and Fragkiadakis; Emmerling; Callonnec;
Pastore and Santoli — RQ1). Regional case studies of coal territories find the opposite at the local scale:
renewables replace a fraction of lost jobs and do not retain the young (García 2023, León; Martin and Broughel,
Upper Silesia; Moreira, Portugal — RQ1/RQ6). The digital transition shows the same pattern: employment grew in
AI-exposed occupations and regions (Albanesi; Neumann — RQ2) while routine and low-skilled workers in
non-metropolitan manufacturing regions lost (Capello 2024; Doorn and Van Vliet — RQ2). The chapter's framing
should be the gap between the two scales, not a single net number.

**C2. Territorial targeting is the recommendation; the EU's territorial instruments are barely studied.** RQ1 and
RQ2 repeatedly recommend targeting support at the regions that lose (Almazán-Gómez; Perdana and Vielle; Ganter;
Calvillo; Guarascio; Zhang; Rijnks), and RQ6 supplies the risk indicators to do it (Vrontisi; McDowall in RQ7).
Yet only one core study evaluates cohesion policy (Christou — RQ3), none addresses smart specialisation, and the
studies that assess the Just Transition Fund and Mechanism find them narrow and unevenly allocated (Crespy and
Munta; Castiella and Cuesta; Siderius; Peretto; Joosse — RQ6). This disconnect between what the literature
recommends and what it evaluates is a finding in itself and links RQ1, RQ2, RQ3 and RQ6.

**C3. Discontent follows job decline, not the absence of spending.** Perceived unfairness tracks regions where
carbon-intensive employment has fallen (Im), generous compensation has not stopped right-wing populism in German
coal areas (Abraham 2019), and contested participation processes left stakeholders dissatisfied with the German
coal laws (Raitbaur). The one causal evaluation of a just-transition agreement on political outcomes finds an
electoral reward for the incumbent in Spanish coal municipalities, driven by union endorsement (Bolet). The design
lesson across RQ6, consistent with the governance strand of RQ1 (Stroud 2015; Dupuis; Bösch) and RQ2 (Ilsøe; Oder
and Béland), is that structured participation with unions as partners, timing that avoids a "death valley" between
closure and replacement, and coverage of groups the instruments miss (subcontractors, women, the young, non-coal
losers) matter more than the headline amount.

**C4. Skills policy is the lever the literature trusts most, with the least evidence on which model works.** Skills
recommendations dominate RQ2 and RQ4 and recur in RQ1 (Ganter; Calvillo; Stoyanova and Harizanova-Bartos) and
RQ6 (Madsen; Martin and Broughel; Kruczek). What is established: digital skills correlate with employment
(Bejaković and Mrnjavac); training and job-creation schemes mitigate polarisation (Doorn and Van Vliet); IT-plus-
technical skill packages in VET improve outcomes (Kiener); ICT enrolments raise employability (Pirvu); broad
capabilities from well-governed VET systems beat narrow "green skills" packages (Stroud 2024; Evans and Stroud;
Chen; Carstensen). What is not: a comparison of initial-education or lifelong-learning models on labour-market
outcomes, which is what RQ4 asks. Two equity findings should temper the enthusiasm: adult education mostly raises
the proficiency of the already educated (Bles) and green degrees can widen regional inequality among graduates
(Cattani).

**C5. The digital transition is studied as a labour-market phenomenon, not as a territorial grievance.** RQ6's
digital half is empty: no core study treats discontent in low-digitalisation territories under a just-transition
frame. The closest evidence is the regional-divergence strand of RQ2 (Guarascio; Zhang; Capello 2024; Suciu;
Reveiu) and the public-preference studies (Lee; Pulkka). The chapter can state this asymmetry: the green transition
has a just-transition policy architecture and a literature evaluating it; the digital transition has neither.

**C6. Immigration is absent from the transition literature.** RQ5 has four studies, one empirical. Migrant labour
fills the low end of the green economy (Gregson); demographic decline makes immigration a stated necessity in
Poland (Kozera); brain drain leaves CEE AI graduates underutilised (Istudor — RQ2). No study estimates the
contribution of mobility to filling green or digital skill gaps or evaluates a mobility instrument.

**C7. Policy evaluation is the main knowledge gap, and RQ7's core studies do not say so.** RQ7's seven studies are
about measuring green jobs, classifying skills and building vulnerability indices. The gap in evaluation has to be
stated from the rest of the corpus: across 170 core studies, the policy evaluations with an identification strategy
are Bolet (electoral effect of a just-transition agreement), Bratta (firm-level effect of a digital investment
incentive), Haywood and Charlier (counterfactual simulations of labour-market policies) and Siderius (allocation of
the JTM). Everything else is description, modelling of scenarios, or stakeholder perception.

**C8. The evidence is geographically skewed.** Germany, the UK, Poland, Italy, Spain and Greece dominate; the UK is
present in every question although outside the EU; Nordic countries appear mainly through Finnish and Swedish
case studies in RQ6; Benelux, Austria, Ireland (except RQ2/RQ4), the Baltics (except Estonia and Lithuania) and
Bulgaria/Romania (except a few CEE studies) are thin. Studies with regional resolution are a minority in every
question. This belongs in the limitations and in RQ7.

## 4. How to present the gaps **[authors]**

- RQ3 and RQ5: write short sections that state the result (the literature recommends territorial targeting and
  says nothing about the instruments; migration is absent) and what the few studies say, rather than padding with
  partially relevant material. A dedicated search for either would probably add a handful of records
  (DECISIONES_METODO, 14-nov).
- RQ6: split the section into fossil-fuel territories (the evidence) and low-digitalisation territories (the
  absence), bringing in the RQ2 regional strand as the closest evidence.
- RQ7: present the measurement gaps from the core studies and the policy-evaluation gap from C7, stating that the
  latter is the authors' reading of the whole corpus, not a finding of the RQ7 studies.
- Discussion: C1 (two scales), C2 (recommendation without evaluation) and C5 (green has a just-transition
  architecture, digital does not) are the three conclusions that the corpus supports most robustly and that
  distinguish this chapter from the 2025 version.
