# RQ3 — Cohesion and smart-specialisation policies for job creation and destruction in the twin transition

Draft synthesis for the chapter (ISABEL), written from `runs/isabel_chapter_v1/report/evidence_by_rq.md`
(run `isabel_chapter_v1`, protocol v2, core studies only). Every statement rests on the abstract of the study
cited, as charted by the model and verified by quote; **check each study against its full text before the claim
goes into the chapter**. Studies: 5 (two of them also core to another question: Bianchi et al. to RQ4, Calvillo
et al. to RQ1).

## 1. What the five studies say

Only one study evaluates cohesion policy as such. Christou et al. (2025) use 2021–2027 cohesion-policy regional
data and expect green investments under the policy to raise GDP and employment, particularly in less developed
regions, while concluding that instruments beyond cohesion policy are needed to reconcile growth and
environmental objectives.

Two studies argue for place-based design without evaluating a cohesion instrument. Bianchi et al. (2024) treat the
just transition as a digital, green and skills transition and conclude that, within a national education
framework, regions should design and implement place-based education and training initiatives suited to their
socio-economic characteristics, with Emilia-Romagna's holistic industrial, social and education policy since 2010
as the case. Atkins (2024) argues that any UK green industrial policy will be place-based, since net zero both
enables and complicates regional and local economic policy, and that green jobs and skills are central to a just
transition amid deindustrialisation and reindustrialisation.

Two studies supply regional evidence that implies a territorial policy. Calvillo et al. (2025) find that the UK
heat-pump rollout creates net jobs, mainly in construction and manufacturing, but that some regions show lower
relative job creation because skills shortages push up labour costs, so regional levelling-up must be coordinated
with jobs and skills planning. Szeles and Simionescu (2021) find across EU regions that reducing the digital
divide and improving advanced e-skills eases the school-to-work transition of young people and strengthens the
employment effects of education and sectoral growth, and conclude that regions should focus on closing the
divide.

## 2. Reading across the studies (for the authors to confirm)

- The evidence base for RQ3 is thin: five core studies, one of which (Christou) addresses cohesion policy directly
  and none of which addresses smart specialisation by name. This is itself the finding: the literature on the
  employment effects of the twin transition is largely silent on the EU's territorial instruments, even though it
  repeatedly recommends territorial targeting (see RQ1, section 2, and RQ2, section 2).
- What the studies agree on is the direction: place-based design of education, training and industrial policy,
  with regions as the implementing level (Bianchi; Atkins; Szeles and Simionescu), and attention to regional
  skills bottlenecks that cap job creation (Calvillo).
- The cross-reading should bring in the regional studies that are core to RQ1 and RQ2 but not to RQ3
  (Almazán-Gómez; Perdana and Vielle; Ganter; Guarascio; Zhang; Rijnks; Crowley and Doran; García; Martin and
  Broughel): they describe where cohesion-type support would be needed, without naming the instrument.
