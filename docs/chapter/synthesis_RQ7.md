# RQ7 — Knowledge gaps in measuring and forecasting job creation and destruction and in evaluating policies

Draft synthesis for the chapter (ISABEL), written from `runs/isabel_chapter_v1/report/evidence_by_rq.md`
(run `isabel_chapter_v1`, protocol v2, core studies only). Every statement rests on the abstract of the study
cited, as charted by the model and verified by quote; **check each study against its full text before the claim
goes into the chapter**. Studies: 7.

## 1. Measuring green jobs and green firms: definitions still unsettled

Three studies address the measurement of green employment itself. Shapira et al. (2013) review UK attempts to
measure green industries and jobs, find that they raise significant conceptual and measurement issues, and propose
a text-mining approach using search-term combinations to identify green-goods companies, tested on UK SMEs.
Sofroniou and Anderson (2020) chart green job growth in Scotland with an inclusive ISCO-08/O*NET-based measure
rather than a "purist" definition, find that this better captures the broader impact of greening on jobs, and call
for more analytical attention to green job quality and to inclusive green job growth. Chiarello et al. (2021)
compare the ESCO classification of skills with the Industry 4.0 literature through text mining, identify potential
data gaps in ESCO, and show that text mining of scientific literature can give policy makers more up-to-date
labour-market intelligence on emerging skills.

## 2. Forecasting and net effects: cross-border and cross-sector effects are usually ignored

Markandya et al. (2016) estimate that the 1995–2009 shift of the EU energy sector towards gas and renewables
created about 530,000 net jobs (0.24 % of 2009 employment), one third through trans-boundary effects within the
EU, with uneven gains and losses across member states; they note that most studies estimate gross future effects
and ignore effects between sectors and countries. Oncioiu et al. (2025) build a composite green labour-market
readiness index for nine EU countries and forecast it to 2040, finding persistent asymmetries between
North-western and South-eastern Europe that are associated with investment, innovation, human capital and
governance quality; they motivate the index by the lack of unified tools to assess member states' preparedness.

## 3. Regional vulnerability and the measurement of a just transition

McDowall et al. (2023) develop a composite regional vulnerability indicator combining the employment share in four
carbon-intensive industries with dimensions of adaptive capacity and resilience, and find that vulnerability to
job losses under 1.5 °C scenarios is regionally concentrated, with some regions combining high exposure, weak
adaptive capacity and high pre-existing unemployment, and that results vary little across transition pathways.
Shapovalova et al. (2025) find in a participatory case study of north-east Scotland that measuring a just
transition needs holistic, context-specific indicators covering jobs and skills, poverty and wellbeing, community
revitalisation and participatory democracy, and that operationalisation is hampered by a lack of proven
methodologies and by the complexity of the data.

## Reading across the studies (for the authors to confirm)

- The gaps the core studies name: (i) no agreed definition of green jobs or green firms, and official
  classifications (ESCO) lag technological change (Shapira; Sofroniou and Anderson; Chiarello); (ii) employment
  forecasts are mostly gross and single-sector, ignoring inter-sector and cross-border effects (Markandya);
  (iii) no unified tools to assess regional or national readiness and vulnerability (McDowall; Oncioiu); (iv) no
  proven methodology to measure a just transition at the local level (Shapovalova).
- The question also asks about gaps in the evaluation of the cited policies. **None of the seven core studies
  addresses policy evaluation.** The gaps on that side have to be stated from the other questions: in RQ1, only a
  handful of studies evaluate an instrument (Bratta in RQ2 is the one firm-level policy evaluation in the corpus;
  Haywood and Charlier simulate counterfactual policies); in RQ4, almost no study compares training models on
  outcomes; in RQ3 and RQ5, the instruments are barely studied.
- A gap the corpus itself reveals, to be stated in the discussion: the geographic coverage of the evidence is
  uneven (Germany, the UK, Italy and Spain dominate; see the notes of RQ1–RQ4), and studies with regional
  resolution are a minority.
